<p class="article-dek">Match facts: Round of 32 - Thursday, July 2, SoFi Stadium, Inglewood, California. Kickoff 12:00 PT / 3 p.m. ET / 8 p.m. UK. Referee: Glenn Nyberg (Sweden). US TV: Fox. Per <a href="https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/49213161/fifa-world-cup-2026-spain-vs-austria-tv-channel-how-watch-kickoff-live-stream-referee-predicted-lineups">ESPN's match preview</a>, the winner meets Portugal or Croatia in the round of 16.</p>

Team news: Nico Williams (adductor) and Yéremy Pino (shoulder) were both [injured against Uruguay](https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/49193963/spain-2026-world-cup-injury-yeremy-pino-nico-williams); both remain with the squad and could return later in the tournament, but neither is expected to be risked here. ESPN's predicted lineups have Spain in a 4-3-3 with Rodri at the base and Lamine Yamal and Álex Baena wide, and Austria in a 4-2-3-1 with Nicolas Seiwald and Xaver Schlager as the double pivot and Sasa Kalajdzic up front, with ESPN reporting doubts over David Alaba and Marko Arnautovic after knocks against Algeria.

Prediction: Spain 2-1, with at least one uncomfortable half.

The evidence points clearly toward Spain. The danger is that World Cup knockout football can make even the better team feel rushed, and Austria's best chance is to make that feeling arrive before Spain have settled into control.

The Spanish conversation around this match has been more anxious than celebratory. [Cadena SER's framing](https://cadenaser.com/nacional/2026/06/29/el-plan-b-de-la-seleccion-espanola-contra-austria-un-cambio-de-estilo-obligado-a-partir-de-un-nombre-propio-cadena-ser/) has been blunt: Spain are still short of their best level even after topping the group. The build-up has circled around familiar worries - whether Luis de la Fuente needs a Plan B, whether Spain can recover its Euro 2024 rhythm, and whether Yamal can turn belief into incision - and Cadena SER reports the squad has [rehearsed penalties](https://cadenaser.com/nacional/2026/07/01/espana-se-cubre-las-espaldas-ante-una-posible-tanda-en-el-mundial-2026-y-ensaya-los-penaltis-antes-de-los-dieciseisavos-ante-austria-cadena-ser/) ahead of the tie. Spain have not won a World Cup knockout match since lifting the trophy in 2010, and their last two World Cups ended in shootout defeats. They are preparing like a favorite that knows control can disappear late.

The statistical case for Spain is short. Across the group stage they roughly doubled Austria's output - about 17 shots and 1.9 expected goals per game to Austria's eight and 1.1 - while conceding nothing at the other end. Austria conceded twice a game. Those numbers say Spain have built the better platform, and they say something harsher about Austria that any honest preview has to absorb.

The harder part is describing Austria without flattening them into either a high-press caricature or a team waiting for chaos. Rangnick's Austria are not obliged to press high for 90 minutes to remain a pressing team. Against Spain, the more realistic version is selective pressure: start from a compact shape, block the central pass into midfield, then jump when the ball is played wide, backwards or into a player receiving with his body closed. Austria can protect space first and still attack the next pass with Rangnick's intensity.

That is the theory. The group stage was rougher on it. Austria were opened three times by an Algeria side that dominated the ball, and were beaten 2-0 by Argentina; only the Jordan game went broadly to plan. Whatever the precise mechanism, a defence that has conceded five in three games gives Spain a second opportunity: not just to play through a press, but to attack a back line that has already been breached repeatedly.

The pressing data complicates the media shorthand from the other direction. Spanish coverage has leaned on [the Rangnick vocabulary](https://as.com/futbol/mundial/asi-es-austria-el-rival-de-espana-en-dieciseisavos-de-final-f202606-n/) - pressing, Red Bull schooling, the Laimer-Sabitzer-Seiwald spine - plus the narrative facts: back at the finals after 28 years, past the group stage for the first time since 1982 (though that year's "second round" was itself a group phase, not a knockout). Yet by the group-stage numbers, Spain pressed harder: opponents got roughly 6.5 passes off before Spain intervened, against 15.6 for Austria. Context cuts both ways - Spain's opponents barely wanted the ball, and Austria's figure is inflated by the Algeria game, while their pressing against Jordan matched Spain's level. The fair conclusion is narrower than the shorthand: this is not simply Spain's passing against Austria's press. It is Spain's territory against Austria's timing.

Austria's attacking identity matters too. The 3-3 draw with Algeria has naturally been told through the drama of the ending: Riyad Mahrez put Austria seconds from elimination before Kalajdzic headed in [the 96th-minute equalizer](https://www.bild.de/sport/fussball/wm-2026-sekunden-vor-dem-aus-last-minute-rettung-fuer-rangnick-6a407dd147f368e955ce8872). Two quieter points sit under the rescue arc. Austria kept committing bodies forward late, and their best open-play moments against Spain are likely to come when Laimer, Sabitzer or Seiwald can turn a regain into an immediate forward pass rather than a clearance. And that equalizer was a header: against a defence yet to concede at this World Cup, set pieces offer Austria a route that does not depend on sustained pressure, and Kalajdzic's aerial threat is real.

For Spain, the tactical problem goes beyond keeping the ball: it is making Austria's triggers arrive late. Rodri - by common Spanish consent below his best in this tournament - has to do more than recycle possession at the base; he has to draw the first Austrian jump and release the midfielders ahead of him into the spaces either side of Austria's line. The same triggers point backward too: if Austria jump on passes into closed-body receivers, the first stress test is Unai Simón and the centre-backs playing through that jump rather than around it. From there, Spain need Yamal in sequences where his marker is not set - a switch of play, an underlap, a quick regain. With Williams and Pino unavailable there is no like-for-like Plan B on the wings; there is Yamal, and there is Baena finding space while the attention sits elsewhere.

Austria's positive plan begins at the other end of the same problem. If they only defend their box, Spain should eventually generate enough pressure. If they can close the centre, force Spain toward the touchline and then spring forward with runners already close to Kalajdzic, Arnautovic (if fit) or Sabitzer, the game changes shape. Their attack can hurt without long spells of possession - provided the first pass after the regain punishes Spain's rest defence.

The watch point: as the first half develops, where are Austria winning or contesting the ball? Early deep regains prove little on their own - Spain often concede sterile territory by design. If Austria's ball-winning stays pinned near their own box, with Spain immediately restarting another attack, the game should bend toward the favorite. If it migrates toward halfway, onto the edges of Spain's circulation, with runners close enough to turn regains into attacks, Rangnick's side will have found the version of the night it needs. And the clock matters: pressing of Austria's kind is hard to sustain for 90-plus minutes, and if the tie stretches, one team has already rehearsed penalties.

This is not a tie about whether Austria can outplay Spain for 90 minutes. It is about whether their selective pressure can make one Spanish hesitation feel larger than the control that came before it.
